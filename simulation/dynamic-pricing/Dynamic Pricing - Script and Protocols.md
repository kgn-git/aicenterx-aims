# Dynamic Pricing — Script and Protocols

Governing sources: the AI Canvas for the initiative **Dynamic Pricing** (`pricing`) and its governance record in `index.html` (`INITIATIVES`, `CANVASES.pricing`, `PEOPLE`, `INIT_SOURCES`, `SOURCING`, `CHANGE`, `BUDGETS`, `WAVES` w3-o2, `CONTROLS` A.5 to A.10, `REGS`, `BOARD_SEATS`, `MEETINGS`, `DECISIONS`, `STAGES`, and the intake form's classifier signals and `tierFrom`; `RISKS`, `INCIDENTS`, `IMPACTS`, `EVIDENCE`, `MODELS`, `SYSTEM_CARDS`, `DIAGRAMS` and `SUPPLIERS` were checked and hold nothing for `pricing`), read as data and not changed. `index.html` is generated by `tools/build.py`; the canvas text is set there and copied here verbatim. Plan and acceptance criteria: GitHub issue #9 (AI Canvas, narrative script and simulation for Dynamic Pricing), under parent issue #1 (shared approach, beat structure, runtime target). Technical contract: `simulation/_template/README.md`. Format and structure only: `simulation/Village Film C - Three Questions - Script and Protocols.md` and the five initiative scripts beside this one (the Retail Credit Risk Scoring script is the model). This is a standalone film. It is **not** a scene in Village Film C and is **not** set in the robot village. It is set where the proposal actually lives today: a commercial office, a meeting-room whiteboard, the intake review and the desk a pricing owner would work at.

Runtime 59 s: open card 4 s → Beat 1 The situation 10 s (5 / 5) → Beat 2 The AI at work 11 s (6 / 5) → Beat 3 What can go wrong 15 s (5 / 5 / 5) → Beat 4 How it is kept safe 15 s (5 / 5 / 5) → close card 4 s. The four beats run 51 s, inside the 45-60 s target of issue #1, and each card runs 4 s (at most 10 s). Every shot is its own scene in `FILM.scenes`; cuts land exactly on shot boundaries. Autoplay, loop, seekable (a pure function of t).

**Status of the use case.** The initiative is at stage *Idea*, tier *Needs review*, disposition *Not started*, value *Not started*. Its summary reads "Early concept for demand-based pricing; awaiting intake and a first risk screen." No data source, model, system card, diagram, supplier, risk, incident, impact assessment, evidence item or board decision is recorded for it, and no board session has reviewed it. So the film shows the use **as proposed**, never as something in use. Beat 2 is a plan on a whiteboard, not a running system. Beat 3 stages the **open questions** a first risk screen would ask, as labelled what-ifs, because there are no recorded risks or incidents to stage. Beat 4 shows the intake gate the proposal has to pass.

All numbers in this document are **design parameters** unless they are labelled as taken from the record. No results are reported anywhere. The hypotheses in Part 2 are pre-registered predictions for an exercise that has not been run. Nothing on screen claims that a risk exists, that a harm has happened, that a check has passed, or that the proposal has any value. People are the prototype's fictional people and roles (`PEOPLE`), plus made-up customers and a faceless, unnamed "rival" seller. No real organisation, brand, product, competitor or person is named or depicted.

## Open points for review

These points should be confirmed by the owners before phase 2, because the film draws them or depends on them.

1. **Who runs intake, and who decides the tier and the gate?** The record says the proposal awaits intake and a first risk screen, and that tier *Needs review* is "held for a human classification decision", but names no person for either. The film seats the AIMS Manager and the DPO with the owner (SA4), leaves the screen to the AI Risk Owner's function, and says only "a person, not the form, decides which rules apply". This script assumes the Idea-to-Discovery gate is a go decision by the business (the sponsor, Sam Duarte, with the owner) that can be taken only once the AIMS Manager has signed off that intake, the tier decision and the first screen are complete. The AIMS Manager, as the second line, confirms the record and can block, but does not decide whether the business goes on. The decision goes to the board only if the tier comes back High or the screen logs a high risk (see Next gate). Owner: Marcus Webb (A.6), with Priya Nandakumar and Sam Duarte.
2. **The seven classifier signals do not cover two of the three issue #9 questions.** Personal data (shot 3.1) maps to "Processes personal data", but nothing on the intake form asks about explaining a price to a customer (shot 3.2) or about shared market data and competition law (shot 3.3). The film therefore shows the first risk screen, not the classifier, asking those questions (shot 4.2). Should the first screen's checklist name them? Owner: Priya Nandakumar (AI Risk Owner), with Tomas Berg.
3. **The collusion question is in issue #9, not in the canvas.** The canvas Data Strategy asks where the demand data "comes from", which is where a shared or third-party market feed would show up, but no block names competitor or shared market data. The film's source line for shot 3.3 cites Data Strategy and calls the question open. Should the canvas name it? Owner: Maria Okafor, with Sam Duarte.
4. **Regulations in the record.** `pricing` lists only `eu-ai-act`, whose `REGS` entry reads "Applies — high-risk (Annex III) obligations"; for a use whose tier is undetermined that wording overstates what is known. If intake finds personal data in the demand signals, GDPR (control A.7) applies, and personalised pricing is profiling, so a DPIA screen (GDPR Art. 35) belongs in the first screen. The law a pricing practitioner meets first is not in `REGS` at all: consumer law on price information (the full price must be shown; an announced price reduction must quote the lowest price of the prior 30 days, under the Price Indication Directive as amended in 2019), the duty to tell consumers when a price is personalised on the basis of automated decision-making (Consumer Rights Directive, as amended in 2019), unfair-practices law, and, for consumer credit, the duty to tell a consumer when an offer is personalised by automated processing (Consumer Credit Directive 2023/2225). Competition law (Art. 101 TFEU) applies to the shared-tool question (shot 3.3). If neither Annex III point in Open point 5 applies and no other signal is set, the EU AI Act puts no high-risk duty on this use: what remains is the AI-literacy duty (Art. 4) and the organisation's own AIMS controls, and the binding law is consumer, data-protection and competition law. The screen should therefore not read "EU AI Act only" in the record as "the AI Act is the main exposure". The film names no law on screen. Owner: Tomas Berg, with Priya Nandakumar (A.10 if a third-party tool or feed is used).
5. **Whether the EU AI Act tier could be High.** Pricing is not, in general, an Annex III area. Two Annex III points could still catch it, depending on the open product scope (Business Problem): point 5(c), risk assessment and pricing in relation to natural persons for life and health insurance; and point 5(b), evaluating the creditworthiness of natural persons or setting their credit score, if the pricing model itself assessed an applicant's creditworthiness to set their rate for a retail credit product. A pricing model that only took a score from the separately governed credit model (`rcr`, tier High) as an input would not become Annex III by that alone, but the credit model's own oversight would then reach into pricing. Prices set from aggregate demand for a product, the same for every buyer, would fall in neither. The film says only that a person decides which rules apply. Owner: Tomas Berg.
6. **Owner and pricing owner.** The accountable owner is the Head of Credit Risk Analytics (Retail Banking) for a Commercial initiative, and the "pricing owner" who would approve every change has no person. Confirm both. Owner: Sam Duarte.
7. **Budget against "nothing is built".** `BUDGETS.pricing` shows 5 spent on technology. The film says nothing is built, which agrees with the canvas and the absence of any model; the spend is read as exploration. Confirm. Owner: Sam Duarte.
8. **On-screen word total.** The template README limits all on-screen text to 220 words. This script counts headlines, support lines and notes (219 words) and reports the source lines separately (58 words), as record citations alongside the fixed overlay. Read strictly, the README would count source lines too, and no initiative film meets 220 that way. Confirm the reading. Owner: issue #1.
9. **Source-line reading grade.** Source lines are record names ("AI Canvas: Business Value; initiative summary") and score above grade 8 (up to 18.2). They are reported, not enforced, as the README does for client-worded lines. Confirm. Owner: issue #1.
10. **The classifier signal "Decides solely automatically, with legal effect" fits pricing only in part.** GDPR Art. 22 covers a decision about a person, based solely on automated processing, with legal or similarly significant effect. A price set from aggregate demand, the same for every buyer, is not a decision about a person, so Art. 22 does not reach it; a personalised price can be, where it has a significant effect on the person, for example by pricing them out. Human approval of every price change (Model Training) is therefore an oversight and commercial control (A.9), not, for demand-based pricing, a legal requirement. The protocol maps Q4 to A.9 first and to the signal only for personalised prices. Owner: Tomas Berg.
11. **Can one pricing owner approve every change?** Model Training says "a pricing owner approves every price change". Pricing teams that run demand-based prices usually approve the rules, the price floors and ceilings and the largest step allowed, then review exceptions, because the number of changes outgrows one person's day. The canvas already leaves this to the gate ("the gate decides whether that holds"), and the film says so in shot 4.3; the screen should ask how many changes a day the scope implies. The same practice answers the margin question: no pricing team lets a model pick the margin-maximising price unbounded. Floors, ceilings, the largest step allowed and how often a price may change are set by people, and intake should name who sets them. The canvas records none of these, so shot 2.1 draws no chosen price. Owner: Sam Duarte, with Marcus Webb.

### Plain-language conventions

1. **Full sentences, never fragments.** Every line has its own subject and verb. The note on shot 3.1 is two short sentences.
2. **People first.** Beat 1 names who has the stake: the staff who review prices by hand and the commercial team that wants the margin. It does not dress the margin problem up as a customer harm, because a lagging price is as often cheaper for the customer. Beat 2 says what the proposal is for (more margin) and is honest that no customer gain is claimed, because the canvas claims none. Beat 3 asks, for each question, who would be harmed: a customer charged differently, a buyer who gets no explanation, buyers left with no cheaper choice. Beat 4 says who acts: intake names, a person decides which rules apply, the screen tests and logs, a pricing owner approves.
3. **Proposed, not in use.** Lines about what the model would do use "would" or "as planned". Lines about what must happen first use "must" or "next". The open card and the top-right label say nothing is built. No line uses the present tense for the model as if it existed.
4. **Questions are called questions.** Every Beat 3 headline is a question, the top-left label begins "What-if", and shot 3.1's note says none of it has happened. No Beat 3 line says a risk exists, and red appears only as a dashed outline.

## Part 2 — Protocol

The film shows a proposal and the gate in front of it. This protocol specifies how the proposal's open questions could be **rehearsed before intake**, so that the first risk screen is tested on cases where the right answer is known. It has two parts. Part A rehearses the screen itself on written proposal variants with seeded issues. Part B is an optional sandbox that gives the screen concrete measures for each question, on a toy pricing rule in a synthetic market. Neither part builds, trains or chooses the pricing model; the Modeling Approach is "not chosen", and this protocol does not choose it. Nothing here has been run.

### Aim

Find out, before intake, whether a first risk screen would catch the three questions issue #9 and the canvas leave open (a price that differs by who the customer is; a price change nobody can explain; prices pulled into line with other sellers' through a pricing tool or data pool they share), plus the canvas's first design question (does the model set prices, or does a person approve them?), when those issues are planted in a proposal on purpose. Find out whether the seven intake classifier signals alone catch them, or whether the screen needs its own questions. And, optionally, give each question a measure the screen could use later, on a synthetic market where the harm can be seen without any real customer or real price.

### Why it matters to people

A price is something every customer meets. If prices follow demand, a customer may pay more on one day than on another, which people broadly accept when demand explains it. They do not accept paying more because of who they are, finding that nobody can say why a price went up, or a market where sellers' prices are lined up through a tool they share, so buyers have no cheaper choice. Prices that rise together because demand rises are not that harm; prices that move together when demand does not explain it can be. Each of these harms falls on people who never see the model. The canvas already says that a price must stay explainable and must not differ by who the customer is, and that the screen must test this before anything is built. A screen that misses these questions would let the proposal through on a form that never asked them.

### AI-safety and governance question

**On screen (Beat 3, verbatim):** *Could two customers be charged more or less for who they are?* · *Could staff tell a buyer why a price went up?* · *Could a pricing tool rivals also use pull our prices into line?*

Concretely:

1. Does a first screen flag a proposal whose demand signals include personal data or a proxy for it (location, device, channel), and route it to the DPO for a DPIA screen (classifier signal "Processes personal data"; A.5, A.7)?
2. Does it flag a proposal with no way to give a reason for a price change (Customer Value; A.8, not started)?
3. Does it flag a proposal that uses a third-party pricing tool, or a data pool carrying rival sellers' prices, that other sellers also use, and route it to legal review (issue #9; A.10)?
4. Does it flag a proposal in which the model sets prices with no person approving them (Modeling Approach; Model Training; A.9)? For a personalised price, does it also tick "Decides solely automatically, with legal effect" (GDPR Art. 22, Open point 10), and for a price the same for every buyer, correctly leave it unticked?
5. Does the screen leave the tier to a person when the scope makes it uncertain (tier *Needs review*), rather than settling it on the form?

### Setting and props

**Part A — Screen rehearsal (a tabletop exercise).**

- **Proposal variants.** Eight short written proposals for "demand-based pricing" (design parameter), each one page, each in the shape of the canvas (the seven blocks) plus the seven intake classifier signals as the proposer would tick them. - **A simulated rival seller** (SA5) with its own toy rule. Both sellers always see the same synthetic public demand; in arm S1 both also take their prices from one shared toy pricing tool that sees both sellers' prices.
- **A mock pricing-owner desk** (the wireframe desk in shot 4.3): a queue where each proposed price change waits for a role-player to approve or reject it, with or without a reason field.

### Procedure

**Part A — Screen rehearsal.**

1. **Before the first session:** the AIMS Manager registers this protocol (variants, answer key, arms, measures, thresholds) and seals the answer key. The ethics review (proposed: Sofia Lindqvist, as external ethics advisor, not as internal audit) approves it. The DPO and the data owner confirm that no real data and no real proposal text is used (P1).
2. **Assignment:** six panels, three per arm, assigned from the registered seed. Each panel screens all eight variants under its one arm, in an order drawn from the seed, so no panel sees the question sheet and then the bare form. Each seeded issue is therefore screened six times per arm (two variants × three panels), and so are the clean and decoy variants and the two open-scope variants.
3. **Screening:** 10 minutes per variant (design parameter). For each variant the panel ticks the classifier signals, names any risk it would log (in plain words, with a proposed owner), names the tier it would assign or marks it "needs a person", and says whether the variant may pass to discovery.
4. **Scoring:** two scorers blind to arm compare each panel's answers with the sealed key. A third resolves disagreements; agreement is reported.

**Part B — Sandbox market (optional).**

5. Runs only if gate P2 is met. Each of the arm combinations below runs 20 times with different seeds over 26 simulated weeks. The role-player pricing owners work a sample of 200 price changes per arm (design parameter) at the mock desk.
6. Logs are exported with checksums. Results go to the intake record and to the first risk screen, whatever they show.

### Conditions and arms

- **Part A, between panels:** arm **F** (the intake form as it is) and arm **F+Q** (the form plus the question sheet), three panels each. Held constant: the variants, the time per variant and the answer key.
- **Part B, one factor at a time against a baseline** (demand-only signals, each seller pricing on its own, approval with a reason field):
  - **B1, signals:** B1− demand-only signals; B1+ demand plus a per-customer feature (Q1).

### Pre-registered hypotheses

Not on screen: the film states no prediction and no result. All thresholds below are design parameters, fixed in the registration before the first session; the AI Risk Owner and the DPO may set stricter ones.

- **H1 (the form alone, Part A):** *If panels use the intake form as it is (arm F), then they will flag Q1, which the signal "Processes personal data" covers, on at least 5 of 6 screenings, but will flag Q2 and Q3, which no signal covers, on fewer than half.* **Falsified if** F flags Q2 or Q3 on half or more of their screenings, or misses Q1 on more than one. - **H2 (the question sheet, Part A):** *If panels use the form plus the question sheet (arm F+Q), then they will flag every seeded issue on at least 5 of 6 screenings, with no more than one false flag across all clean and decoy screenings, and will pass no variant that carries a seeded issue.* **Falsified if** any of these fails.
- **H3 (the tier, Part A):** *In both arms, panels will leave the tier to a person on at least 5 of the 6 screenings of the two open-scope variants.* **Falsified otherwise.** This tests the *Needs review* path, not a tier for `pricing`.
